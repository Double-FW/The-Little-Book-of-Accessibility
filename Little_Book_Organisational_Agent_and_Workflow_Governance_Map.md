# The Little Book of Accessibility: organisational agent and workflow alignment

## 1. Purpose and central finding

The Little Book is most valuable as a source of organisational judgement: it helps agents ask better questions, identify exclusion, translate intent into delivery expectations and examine whether evidence supports a claim. Its strongest applications are strategic advice, role support, design reasoning, management reporting and sustained organisational change.

Its value should extend across the agents an organisation already uses. An accessibility adviser alone cannot preserve inclusion when a portfolio agent prioritises funding, a delivery agent changes scope, a procurement agent evaluates a supplier or a reporting agent summarises results. Those agents need the relevant principles at the point where their work can affect participation.

This document maps all 16 files in the repository's **Knowledge Base Documents** directory to proposed agent functions and workflow decisions. It also identifies supporting public Double-FW resources. The mappings are an analytical recommendation derived from the sources, rather than a claim that the repository defines these agent types or that an organisation has approved them.

“Agent” means an AI assistant or software agent performing a defined organisational function, whether conversational, embedded in a business system or coordinating a workflow. A function does not require a separate agent: one assistant may perform several functions, and several agents may share one function.

Use this document to decide:

- Which knowledge an agent needs and when to retrieve it.
- Which decisions the knowledge should inform.
- What evidence and handover the agent should produce.
- Which human role owns the decision and outcome.
- Which specialist source should supplement the Little Book.

This is governance guidance. It is not an implementation specification, a compliance test library, an operational approval or a delegation of decision authority.

## 2. Shared reasoning foundation

All agent functions should preserve four foundations, drawing principally on [S01], [S02] and [R01]:

1. **Define the designed experience.** Identify the intended activity, people, context and visual/non-visual pathways. “Everyone” needs an explicit scope; it is not evidence of universal coverage.
2. **Examine emotional, functional and technical outcomes.** Completion alone does not establish independence, dignity, confidence, sustainable effort or satisfaction.
3. **Treat barriers as design and organisational responsibilities.** Consider content, interfaces, systems, incentives, service arrangements and environments, alongside individual preferences and access needs.
4. **Separate intention, activity and demonstrated outcome.** A commitment, training session, generated design, completed ticket or passed automated check answers a different question from whether people can participate.

Comparable experience means a fair opportunity to achieve the intended outcome with appropriate quality, control, dignity and effort. It does not require identical interfaces, techniques or completion times. Disabled people's participation supplies knowledge and challenge; agent-generated personas or simulated users cannot replace it.

The Little Book's POUR + Satisfaction framing is a management and experience lens. It does not add a normative principle to WCAG or establish legal compliance. [S04] and [S07] support outcome discussions; any normative decision needs its own applicable, verified source.

## 4. Agent functions, work products and human accountability

The owner roles below are recommendations for local assignment. They do not imply that the named roles already exist or have approved these arrangements.

## 5. Placement in production and management workflows

Retrieve guidance **before the relevant decision is committed**, then revisit it when evidence, scope or context changes. This is the point of greatest influence; a review after a launch or contract can still help, but has fewer options.

| Workflow point and trigger | Agent functions and sources | Valuable questions and evidence | Decision or handover |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Strategy and operating-model review** — new ambition, annual planning or recurring failure | Strategy, governance, transformation; [S03], [S06], [S09] | Which commitment becomes a repeatable practice? Who can act, and what capability or incentive is missing? | Proposed commitments, ownership, resources and outcomes to executive sponsor. |
| **Investment and portfolio prioritisation** — funding request or competing initiatives | Portfolio, strategy, reporting; [S08], [S01], [S11] | Which activities are blocked? Are benefits evidenced, forecast or unverified? What consequence follows deferral? | Options and participation impacts to investment owner; retain unresolved barriers. |
| **Discovery and research commissioning** — new service, redesign or uncertain audience | Product, research, design; [S02], [S01] lessons 6–8, [S15] sections 3–4 | Who is missing from insight? Can people participate in the research itself? What contexts and pathways matter? | Audience/task definition, accessible research plan and assumptions to research/product owners. |
| **Procurement and AI intake** — shortlist, pilot, renewal or material supplier change | Procurement, AI governance, HR, architecture; [S03], [S05], [S01] lesson 26 | Can intended users perform real tasks? What evidence is supplier-generated? What support, data flow or integration needs review? | Evidence requests, local evaluation and unresolved contractual/governance decisions to owners. |
| **Briefs, architecture and requirements** — before pattern/platform choices are fixed | Product, design, architecture, content; [S11], [S13]–[S15] | What must people understand, control and recover from? | **Implementation and integration** — component assembly, state change or service handoff | Engineering, design, QA; [S01] lessons 20 and 24, [S14], [S15] sections 14–18 | Has implementation preserved structure, feedback, recovery and transitions? Does component composition create new barriers? | Evidence and integration gaps back to product/design owners and test planning. |
| **Scope change and deadline pressure** — proposed feature removal, waiver or deferral | Delivery, governance, portfolio; [S11], [S01] lessons 13 and 28 | Whose participation changes? Is an alternative usable and private? Who has authority to decide, and when will the barrier be reviewed? | Impact/options/recovery record to authorised decision-maker. | **Pre-release or pre-publication review** — complete journey or content ready for assessment | QA, release, research, reporting; [S01] lessons 21–23, [S15] section 18 | What was actually tested, for whom, in which environment? What is still proposed or unknown? | Bounded assurance statement, known barriers and decision requests to release/editorial owner. |
| **Operation, support and incident review** — complaint, recurring assistance, regression or changed context | Operations, analytics, HR, product; [S04], [S06], [S01] lessons 26 and 28 | Can the person achieve the goal now? | **Management/ESG reporting** — board pack, statement or public claim | Analytics, reporting, governance; [S07], [S09], [S01] lesson 27 | Are claims current and supported? Are averages hiding barriers? Are plans funded and actions completed? | Qualified claim/evidence map for human approval and follow-up decisions. |
| **Change adoption and knowledge renewal** — new patterns, technology, roles or lessons | Transformation, learning, knowledge steward; [S06], [S11], [S01] maintenance sections | Did teams change practice? Are sources consistent and current? Are improvements experienced rather than merely documented? | Revised learning, patterns and retrieval guidance with owner, rationale and review status. |

Early disabled participation is relevant to problem framing, co-design and governance. Address known avoidable design and technical failures before asking people to evaluate a developed experience, while continuing earlier research into unresolved questions. “Test later” must not become “involve disabled people only at the end.” [S01], “Bring the right evidence into the right decision”.

## 7. Retrieval and handover governance

### Select a small, contextual source set

Give an agent access to the source map rather than loading every document into every task. Use [S01] and [S02] for the shared framing, then retrieve the relevant role source and specialist supplement. Keep the parent section, “Evidence and limits”, examples and qualifications together when they affect interpretation.

For example, a procurement agent comparing payroll systems should retrieve [S01] lesson 26, [S03] supplier governance and [E06]. A creative brief may need [S10], [S12] and [S01] lessons 15–17 instead. [S16] should be retrieved only for its named organisational context.

Store source identity, repository path, revision and relevant heading with the advice. Repeated statements in overlapping files are not independent corroboration. Check for renamed files and changed content before refreshing an approved knowledge package.

### Preserve meaning at agent handoffs

Each handover should contain:

| Field | What to retain |
| --- | --- |
| Decision and task | The organisational question and the activity people need to complete. |
| People, pathways and context | Relevant user variability, input/output methods, environment and scope limitations. |
| Intended outcome | Completion, understanding, control, independence, dignity, effort and satisfaction as applicable. |
| Evidence and status | Observation, reported experience, proposed option or tested result; method, date, coverage and limits. |
| Sources and authority | File, heading, revision and whether the statement is guidance, approved policy or another requirement. |
| Open issues | Barriers, conflicts, unknowns and consequences; avoid presenting unknown as pass. |
| Action and ownership | Next action, receiving human owner, decision required and review point. |
| Data handling | Controlled evidence references and only the minimum necessary personal information. |

The receiving agent should preserve an unresolved issue until evidence or an authorised decision changes its status. A summary must not turn “proposed alternative” into “validated route”, or “commitment” into “delivered practice”.

## 9. Local adoption and review

An organisation can adopt this mapping through a proportionate governance review:

1. **Name the functions and owners.** Map these functions to existing assistants, workflows and human roles; avoid creating separate agents without a useful operational reason.
2. **Select and approve source packages.** Record scope, source revisions, relevant headings, status, conflicts and review ownership. Confirm rights for the intended reuse separately.
3. **Define permissions independently.** Reading this knowledge supports advice; publishing, changing systems, accepting risk, making employment decisions or committing spend requires separate organisational authority.
4. **Validate decision support.** Review representative scenarios, including scope pressure, unsupported claims, missing research, privacy-sensitive data and conflicting sources. Check the quality of reasoning, routing and uncertainty as well as the finished wording.
5. **Monitor outcomes and renew.** Revisit the mapping after source changes, new agent functions, reorganisations, incidents, recurring barriers or material changes in service use.

Useful adoption measures include whether advice changes decisions, whether handoffs preserve unresolved barriers, whether claims retain evidence limits, whether owners act on findings and whether affected people experience improvement. Retrieval counts, agent output volume and training attendance are supporting activity measures.

Do not make sensitive disability-data collection a prerequisite for using this guidance. Where outcome segmentation is justified, specify purpose, voluntary disclosure arrangements where applicable, access, retention, small-group protections and reporting limits through the organisation's approved processes.

## 10. Worked governance examples

### A. Customer onboarding redesign

The product assistant uses [S02] to define the complete activity. The design assistant uses [S13] and [S15] to examine memory demands, feedback and non-visual/input pathways. The research assistant uses [S01] lessons 6–8 to plan accessible participation and report gaps.

A delivery agent then proposes removing a recovery feature to meet a deadline. [S11] prompts an impact review covering lost work, repeated effort, alternatives and the decision owner. QA distinguishes confirmed implementation results from participant findings. The reporting assistant describes remaining gaps and funded actions rather than saying onboarding is accessible because components passed checks.

### B. Internal HR-system procurement

The procurement assistant combines [S01] lesson 26 with [E06] to define representative work goals and realistic access-technology configurations. HR and research plan evaluation of independence, accuracy, fatigue and cumulative burden.

If a supplier provides a conformance report, the assistant records its scope and requests evidence relevant to the actual tasks. It routes unresolved system barriers through [E11] and data questions through [E03]. Humans decide procurement and support arrangements; the agent does not attribute system-induced difficulty to employee competence.

### C. AI programme expansion and board reporting

The AI governance assistant uses [S05] and [E07] to identify evidence gaps in representation, accessibility, capability and oversight. The change assistant uses [S06] to connect these gaps to systems, skills, ownership and leadership practice.

A board-reporting assistant uses [S07] to distinguish planned safeguards from demonstrated outcomes. [E01] supports a delivery record with missing baselines or targets left explicitly unknown. The board receives options, evidence and decisions required, rather than an invented maturity rating or a claim that automation is already inclusive.
