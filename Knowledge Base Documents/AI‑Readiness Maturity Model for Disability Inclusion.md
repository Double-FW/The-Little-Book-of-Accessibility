# AI‑Readiness Maturity Model for Disability Inclusion

### _A five‑level framework for assessing and improving ethical AI adoption in relation to disability inclusion._

This model evaluates an organisation across **six dimensions**:

1.  **Leadership & Governance**
2.  **Data & Representation**
3.  **Accessibility & Inclusive Design**
4.  **People, Skills & Culture**
5.  **Technology & Infrastructure**
6.  **Ethics, Safeguards & Risk Management**

Each dimension is assessed across **five maturity levels**.

# AWARENESS

### _“We know AI exists, but we haven’t connected it to disability inclusion.”_

### Characteristics:

*   Leadership has limited understanding of AI’s impact on disabled people.
*   No disability‑specific AI policies or governance.
*   Data on disability is incomplete, inconsistent, or not used.
*   Accessibility is reactive, not embedded.
*   Teams lack AI literacy and disability inclusion skills.
*   No safeguards for bias, harm, or exclusion.

### Risks:

*   AI tools introduced informally without oversight.
*   High likelihood of bias or exclusion.
*   Disabled employees and customers are invisible in AI decisions.

### What organisations must do next:

*   Build foundational awareness of AI risks and opportunities.
*   Establish basic accessibility and inclusion principles.
*   Begin mapping where AI is already being used informally.

# FOUNDATIONS

### _“We have policies and intentions, but limited operational capability.”_

### Characteristics:

*   Leadership acknowledges AI’s relevance to inclusion.
*   Early governance structures exist but are not disability‑specific.
*   Some accessibility standards are in place but inconsistently applied.
*   Disability data collection is improving but still incomplete.
*   Teams have basic training in AI ethics or accessibility.
*   AI tools are procured with minimal accessibility checks.

### Risks:

*   Policies exist on paper but not in practice.
*   Accessibility is still treated as a compliance task.
*   Disabled people are consulted occasionally, not systematically.

### What organisations must do next:

*   Formalise disability‑specific AI governance.
*   Introduce accessibility requirements into procurement.
*   Begin auditing AI systems for bias and accessibility.

# OPERATIONALISING

### _“We are actively embedding disability inclusion into AI workflows.”_

### Characteristics:

*   Clear governance for AI with disability inclusion embedded.
*   Accessibility is part of product, HR, and service design processes.
*   Disability data is collected ethically, transparently, and with consent.
*   AI systems undergo regular bias and accessibility audits.
*   Disabled people are involved in testing and feedback loops.
*   Teams have practical skills in inclusive design and responsible AI.

### Risks:

*   Inconsistent implementation across business units.
*   Gaps between policy and lived experience.
*   Over‑reliance on automated tools without human oversight.

### What organisations must do next:

*   Standardise inclusive AI practices across all teams.
*   Build cross‑functional accessibility and AI ethics councils.
*   Expand lived‑experience involvement into co‑design roles.

# INTEGRATED

### _“AI, accessibility, and disability inclusion are fully integrated into operations.”_

### Characteristics:

*   AI governance includes disabled people in decision‑making roles.
*   Accessibility is embedded into every stage of the AI lifecycle.
*   Data pipelines are transparent, ethical, and representative.
*   AI systems proactively detect and mitigate bias.
*   Teams use AI to enhance accessibility (e.g., alt text, captioning, AT compatibility).
*   Procurement requires demonstrable accessibility and fairness from vendors.

### Risks:

*   Scaling AI faster than governance can keep up.
*   Overconfidence in automated bias detection.

### What organisations must do next:

*   Move from “inclusive AI” to “AI that actively advances inclusion”.
*   Build predictive models that identify barriers before they occur.
*   Share best practice externally to influence industry standards.

# TRANSFORMATIVE

### _“AI is used to actively advance disability inclusion, innovation, and equity.”_

### Characteristics:

*   Disabled people co‑lead AI strategy, governance, and innovation.
*   AI systems are designed to enhance autonomy, access, and participation.
*   Accessibility is treated as a driver of innovation, not a constraint.
*   AI is used to personalise support, remove barriers, and predict needs.
*   The organisation contributes to global standards for ethical, inclusive AI.
*   Continuous improvement is built into every AI system.

### Risks:

*   Becoming a target for scrutiny due to leadership position.
*   Need for constant vigilance as AI evolves.

### What organisations must do next:

*   Maintain transparency and accountability.
*   Continue co‑creation with disabled communities.
*   Influence policy, regulation, and industry norms.

# MATURITY DIMENSIONS (DETAILED)

Below is a quick view of how each dimension evolves across the five levels.

| Dimension | Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 | Level 4 | Level 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Leadership & Governance | No AI strategy | Basic policies | Disability aware governance | Fully integrated governance | Disabled people co‑lead AI strategy |
| Data & Representation | Minimal data | Improving data | Ethical, consent‑based data | Representative datasets | Community‑validated datasets |
| Accessibility & Inclusive Design | Reactive | Partial standards | Embedded in workflows | Fully integrated | AI enhances accessibility |
| People, Skills & Culture | Low literacy | Basic training | Skilled teams | Organisation‑wide capability | Culture of inclusive AI innovation |
| Technology & Infrastructure | Ad‑hoc tools | Early controls | Audited systems | Proactive bias detection | Predictive, inclusive AI |
| Ethics, Safeguards & Risk | No safeguards | Basic safeguards | Regular audits | Continuous monitoring | Industry‑leading ethical practice |
