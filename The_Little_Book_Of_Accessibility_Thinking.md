# Little Book of Accessibility Thinking

## Purpose and use

This knowledge base explores how organisations think about inclusion, make decisions and deliver experiences that people can use with dignity, confidence and independence. Its starting point is lessons learned from managing an accessibility and inclusive design programme over 18 years. It is intended for accessibility, design and engineering managers, as well as the people with whom they share responsibility for delivery.

The focus is organisational judgement and lived experience. Technical requirements and formal assurance contribute to delivery, but they cannot describe the whole experience of participating, enjoying content, completing work or belonging to an audience.

The explanations, managerial applications and examples in this revision expand the supplied lessons. They are guidance for applying those lessons, rather than claims that every suggested practice was used in the original programme. Illustrative scenarios are not research findings.

### How to interpret the guidance

- **Principle:** the underlying position that should guide decisions.
- **Managerial application:** practical ways to apply the principle. Select and adapt these to the product, organisation and people involved.
- **Evidence and limits:** what would support a conclusion and what should not be inferred.
- **Must:** a necessary condition within the stated scope, such as making participation possible or being honest about a known barrier.
- **Should:** the recommended approach, with any departure explained.
- **May:** an option whose value depends on context.

No numerical quotas, participant counts, persona counts or percentage improvements are prescribed here. Establish any local targets from the intended audience, research question, baseline and consequences of exclusion. Explain their rationale and review them when circumstances change.

A lesson retrieved on its own should be interpreted with its scope and limitations. Do not turn an example into a universal requirement, infer that an unmentioned group is unimportant, or claim that a process guarantees inclusion.

## 1. Accessibility as emotion and function

**Principle.** Accessibility concerns both what people can do and how participation makes them feel. An experience may permit task completion while leaving someone anxious, conspicuous, dependent or unwelcome. Those are relevant design outcomes, not incidental reactions outside the team's responsibility.

**Managerial application.** Discuss confidence, dignity, belonging and control alongside task completion. Ask whether people can find the accessible route without disclosing personal information, whether assistance is optional, and whether different ways of participating carry equal status. For example, a service that requires a disabled person to telephone for every transaction may provide access to an outcome while removing the convenience and privacy available to others.

**Evidence and limits.** Combine observations of task performance with people's accounts of confidence, effort and treatment. Do not assume emotional outcomes from a team's good intentions or infer satisfaction from successful completion. Prioritise changes according to demonstrated needs; there is no requirement to manufacture an improvement in every dimension for every release.

## 2. Language of accessibility and avoiding otherness

**Principle.** Organisational language shapes who is imagined as an ordinary user. Describing accessibility as something added for a separate category of people can reinforce the idea that disabled people sit outside the intended audience.

**Managerial application.** Replace language such as “we add accessibility for people who need it” with language that assigns responsibility to normal delivery: “We design and deliver our services so that disabled people can participate alongside the rest of our audience.” Discuss inclusion in planning, brand, service quality and customer experience, while naming specific barriers and responsibilities when precision is needed.

**Evidence and limits.** Look for changes in decisions, budgets and ownership as well as wording. Mainstreaming accessibility must not make disabled people invisible or remove the language needed to describe discrimination, identity or specific requirements. Inclusive wording without operational change is a communication exercise, not evidence of inclusion.

## 3. Inclusion language in organisational strategy

**Principle.** Commitments to “everyone”, “diversity” and “inclusion” encompass disabled people. These words provide a basis for asking whether organisational behaviour matches declared values.

**Managerial application.** Translate broad commitments into responsibilities at decision points: who considers inclusion in business cases, research recruitment, procurement, design and release? Define what participation means for the intended audience and which outcomes will be examined. A promise that a workplace system supports all staff should lead to examination of the tasks those staff actually need to perform.

**Evidence and limits.** Track relevant outcomes and gaps between promises and delivery. A mission statement expresses a commitment; it does not establish that all products already deliver it. Avoid universal recruitment quotas or blanket process measures detached from research purpose. When an ambition cannot yet be met, make the gap, its consequences and the improvement work explicit.

## 4. Games, media and social currency

**Principle.** Games and media are shared cultural experiences. Exclusion can remove both access to content and the opportunity to join conversations, communities and relationships around it.

**Managerial application.** Consider what makes the experience meaningful: following the story, understanding humour, competing with friends, contributing to a shared activity or reacting at the same time as other people. Choose interaction and presentation options that support those purposes. Captions, adaptable controls and alternative information channels are possible responses, but their relevance depends on the experience and the barriers identified.

**Evidence and limits.** Evaluate participation in realistic social contexts as well as isolated task success. Ask whether an alternative route preserves the intended experience and social opportunity. A transcript or summary may convey information without reproducing timing, suspense or shared participation. Explain such differences rather than describing any alternative as automatically equivalent.

## 5. Building accessible things: early and continuous inclusion

**Principle.** Move from repairing inaccessible outputs to making inclusion part of how outputs are conceived, designed, built and maintained. Early decisions influence the cost and feasibility of later choices.

**Managerial application.** Include accessibility in problem framing, research methods, architecture, brand decisions and component selection. Translate known needs into relevant acceptance criteria before implementation. Support teams with usable patterns, implementation guidance and appropriate evaluation methods. Continue to examine the experience after release, when new content, integrations and real use may reveal additional barriers.

**Evidence and limits.** Examine late rework, recurring problems, delivery confidence and user outcomes against a meaningful baseline. Early investment can reduce avoidable work, but cost savings are not guaranteed for every project and should not be invented. A rise in reported barriers may reflect improved detection. “Shift left” means start earlier and continue throughout delivery; it does not mean finish accessibility before development begins.

## 6. Research participation and missing disabled voices

**Principle.** Research methods influence whose experiences become visible. A recruitment route, consent process, venue or platform that prevents participation can systematically omit important insight.

**Managerial application.** Examine the complete participation journey: finding the study, responding, understanding the invitation, giving consent, attending, using materials and receiving payment. Offer suitable participation methods and ask about access needs without assuming them from a diagnosis. Choose participants whose experiences help answer the research question, including relevant disabled people and assistive technology users.

**Evidence and limits.** Record the intended sampling approach, recruitment barriers, people or contexts not reached, and the implications for conclusions. Population estimates do not describe every product audience or determine the correct sample for every study. Removing some participants also cannot be translated into a precise percentage of “lost insight”. Make evidence gaps visible rather than presenting the remaining sample as complete.

## 7. Demographics, identity and human characteristics

**Principle.** Diagnosis alone does not predict how a person will use a product. Useful design insight comes from activities, environments, preferences, capabilities, strategies and barriers, informed by relevant lived experience.

**Managerial application.** Ask how participants accomplish tasks, what they adapt, when assistance becomes necessary and what creates effort or uncertainty. Retain relevant self-described identity and disability context, rather than replacing all demographic information with a new set of behavioural labels. Different people with the same diagnosis may need different approaches; people with different diagnoses may encounter a similar interaction barrier.

**Evidence and limits.** Use functional needs to explain design recommendations and identity information to understand representation or unequal outcomes where relevant. Do not infer diagnoses, equate neurodivergence with a single capability profile, or require disclosure without a clear purpose. Participation and disclosure should be voluntary, with suitable privacy protections and honest reporting of missing information.

## 8. Individual accounts, qualitative evidence and generalisation

**Principle.** Individual accounts and observed events can provide valuable qualitative evidence. Their value and limitations depend on the question being asked. Evidence that one person encountered a barrier is different from evidence about how common that barrier is.

**Managerial application.** Record what was reported or observed, the context and the interpretation separately. Use accounts to investigate barriers and form hypotheses about causes or wider effects. A reproducible failure may justify immediate correction without waiting for more people to experience it. Claims about prevalence, comparative performance or a preferred design direction require evidence suited to those claims.

**Evidence and limits.** Do not impose a fixed number of confirmations before treating an observation as evidence. Structured qualitative research is not inadequate merely because it does not estimate statistical significance. Equally, a memorable account cannot establish that an approach works for everyone. Preserve uncertainty, contradictory findings and the distinction between testimony, observation, interpretation and generalisation.

## 9. Accessibility culture and leadership

**Principle.** Accessibility outcomes are shaped by organisational culture: what leaders reward, what teams can challenge, whose experience counts and what happens under pressure. Leadership matters, while responsibility for implementation remains distributed.

**Managerial application.** Give decision-makers clear responsibilities, time, capability and routes for resolving barriers. Use champions networks to connect teams, share practical learning and make concerns visible. Support specialists where specialist judgement is needed. Managers should examine whether commercial pressure repeatedly displaces inclusion and whether staff can raise an issue without being treated as obstructive.

**Evidence and limits.** Training completion, meeting attendance and network size describe activities. They do not establish that teams can deliver inclusive experiences. Look for decisions changing, repeated barriers being prevented and concerns reaching people with authority to act. Champions need support and recognised capacity; they must not become an unpaid substitute for accountable delivery or specialist expertise.

## 10. Emotional, functional and technical accessibility

**Principle.** Emotional, functional and technical accessibility are interdependent dimensions of an inclusive experience. Strength in one does not compensate for a serious barrier in another.

- **Emotional:** Does participation preserve dignity, confidence, belonging and a sense of control?
- **Functional:** Can people understand and complete the intended activity accurately, independently and with sustainable effort in relevant conditions?
- **Technical:** Does implementation expose the intended information, structure and interaction reliably through the platforms and access technologies people use?

**Managerial application.** Use these questions in design discussions, evaluation and prioritisation. Identify which dimension a proposed change addresses and whether it creates consequences elsewhere. For example, a technically operable alternative may still be difficult to discover or socially isolating.

**Evidence and limits.** Evaluate each relevant dimension using suitable methods. “Balanced” does not mean equal scores, equal investment or permission to trade dignity against functionality. Nor should the framework become a release quota: improvements should respond to needs and risks.

## 11. Temporary, situational and environmental constraints

**Principle.** Interaction needs vary with health, fatigue, location, equipment and circumstances. Designing for varied conditions can extend the usefulness of inclusive choices beyond the people originally considered.

**Managerial application.** Explore contexts such as using a device one-handed, listening in noise, reading with reduced brightness or working while fatigued. Identify what changes in the task and provide suitable options: captions, adaptable presentation, clear interaction, recoverable errors or reduced reliance on a single input method.

**Evidence and limits.** These examples reveal overlapping needs; they do not make temporary inconvenience equivalent to living with disability or the social barriers associated with it. Test relevant contexts rather than prescribing universal defaults such as always-on captions or a single night mode. A feature can benefit many people while remaining particularly important to disabled users. Broader benefit should strengthen the case for inclusion without becoming its condition.

## 12. Designing for the reality in which people live

**Principle.** Design should reflect actual contexts rather than idealised users, equipment or surroundings. A successful demonstration in a controlled environment does not establish success in everyday use.

**Managerial application.** Investigate the conditions that materially affect the intended activity: device capability, connection, interruptions, lighting, noise, fatigue, access technology and support arrangements. Use field research, realistic simulation or other suitable methods. Explain why those methods illuminate the research question and make the research setting itself accessible and safe for participants.

**Evidence and limits.** There is no universal percentage of sessions that must take place outside a research room. An internal payroll task and an outdoor travel task require different contextual coverage. Document what was examined and what remains uncertain. Controlled sessions can reveal valuable detail; contextual evaluation helps establish how that detail applies in practice.

## 13. Audience scope, exclusion and decision accountability

**Principle.** Ask early who could be excluded and why. Defining an intended audience is different from accepting barriers that prevent people within that audience from participating.

**Managerial application.** Use “Who are we willing to exclude?” as a challenge to assumptions, not permission to remove inconvenient needs. Separate a purpose-based scope decision, such as a service intended for a particular age group, from an access barrier, such as a task that cannot be completed using a required input method. Record the rationale, affected people, alternatives and decision owner.

**Evidence and limits.** Scope may change when new evidence emerges. Any narrowing of participation should be explicit and reviewed rather than quietly introduced by implementation constraints. Documentation does not make exclusion acceptable. Examine whether a proposed limit is necessary, whether a different approach would avoid it, and what practical consequences remain for people who cannot participate.

## 14. Social-model thinking in design

**Principle.** Social-model thinking directs attention towards barriers created by environments, systems and organisational practices. It helps teams examine their responsibility instead of treating exclusion as an inevitable consequence of someone's impairment.

**Managerial application.** Describe the interaction that prevents participation: an audio-only announcement, a process dependent on unaided memory or a workplace tool that requires repeated assistance. Ask what could change in the design, service or support arrangements. Include disabled people's own explanations of the experience rather than reducing them to a checklist of limitations.

**Evidence and limits.** An impairment can be relevant to understanding an interaction without making the person responsible for the barrier. Design choices can actively create exclusion even when nobody intends that outcome. The phrase “inaccessibility is proactive” should therefore be understood as responsibility for design consequences, not an assumption that every failure is deliberate.

## 15. Text alternatives, ownership and editorial intent

**Principle.** A text alternative should communicate the purpose, meaning or function of an image in its context. Responsibility should sit with people who understand that purpose, with technical support to implement the decision correctly.

**Managerial application.** Identify an accountable owner for each class of content. Marketing may own brand imagery, content teams editorial imagery and designers functional icons, but adapt this allocation to the organisation. Determine whether the image contributes meaning, repeats nearby information, performs an action or is decorative. A complex graphic may need a concise introduction plus a fuller explanation or underlying information. A logo's treatment depends on its context, including whether it identifies an organisation or acts as a link.

**Evidence and limits.** Review whether people receive the intended information or action without unnecessary repetition. Every image needs an appropriate treatment decision, not necessarily a spoken description. Decorative imagery may appropriately have an empty text alternative. Naming objects alone may miss editorial purpose; purpose alone may also omit important information.

## 16. Bi-media production and audio description

**Principle.** Planning a spoken narrative that makes sense without pictures can improve access to essential meaning and reduce reliance on later explanation. This is particularly useful where a format can naturally support clear verbal communication.

**Managerial application.** Review scripts for information that is available only visually: who is speaking, what changes, an instruction shown on screen or a significant action. Decide what can be integrated naturally into speech and what needs additional description. Review the finished production as well as the script, because editing and delivery may alter the result.

**Evidence and limits.** A self-contained spoken narrative is not a guarantee of an accessible production for every audience. Some visual qualities, performances or dramatic choices need a separate descriptive approach, and other audiences need other access provision. Judge the result by understanding and experience, not by maximising the proportion of content produced without additional description. Avoid treating reduced description as the objective in itself.

## 17. Caption experience and composition

**Principle.** Captions should support access to speech and relevant sound while preserving access to faces, actions and graphics. Their relationship to the picture is part of the experience.

**Managerial application.** Consider overlapping patterns of use: people who consult captions when speech is unclear; lip readers who consult them when visual speech is unavailable; and people who read captions as their primary source before looking at the picture. Plan composition, graphics and caption positioning together. Assess readability and obstruction across relevant display sizes, aspect ratios and presentation options.

**Evidence and limits.** These patterns are not exhaustive or mutually exclusive categories. A person may move between them. A fixed lower-third caption zone cannot guarantee that mouths or important information remain visible. Establish zones suitable for the production, adjust placement where needed and evaluate the combined experience. Include timing, speaker identification and the effort of moving attention between text and picture, not just whether a caption technically fits.

## 18. Affordances, signifiers and conventions

**Principle.** People use prior experience and perceptible cues to predict how an interface works. Familiar patterns can reduce learning, uncertainty and memory demands.

**Managerial application.** Distinguish what an element allows someone to do from the cues that communicate that possibility. Use clear labels, states and feedback, and respect relevant platform conventions. When proposing an unfamiliar interaction, explain the problem it solves and compare it with a familiar alternative through suitable evaluation. Include discovery, errors, recovery and repeated use.

**Evidence and limits.** Conventions are useful starting points, not proof of accessibility. They vary across platforms, cultures and user experience, and some familiar patterns still exclude people. Innovation may be worthwhile, but its claimed benefit should be examined alongside its learning cost and distribution of outcomes. Improved average speed does not justify an otherwise avoidable barrier for a particular group.

## 19. Typography and reading experience

**Principle.** Typeface selection contributes to readability, but the reading experience also depends on size, spacing, line length, rendering, contrast, language, content and the ability to adjust presentation.

**Managerial application.** Consider the real reading task: scanning labels, understanding instructions or sustaining attention through an article. Examine relevant letterform distinctions and how candidate typefaces behave in actual interfaces and content. Test representative material and allow suitable user preferences. Brand identity should be expressed through choices that support the purpose of the text.

**Evidence and limits.** Reading speed, error rate, comprehension, comfort and preference can reveal different strengths. There may be no single typeface that performs best for every person or task. Do not assume a font is accessible because it is marketed that way, or expect spacing adjustments to rescue every unsuitable display face. Evaluate the full typographic system rather than certifying a font in isolation.

## 20. HTML structure as experienced interaction

**Principle.** Structure, semantics, names, relationships and interaction behaviour shape the experience of people who navigate through assistive technology. They are design decisions expressed through implementation.

**Managerial application.** Designers and engineers should agree information hierarchy, meaningful reading order, keyboard operation, focus behaviour and feedback. Express these intentions in specifications and review the working implementation together. Use native platform behaviour where appropriate and examine custom interactions carefully. Treat non-visual navigation as part of the product experience rather than a late technical check.

**Evidence and limits.** A visual prototype may not expose enough structure to support meaningful screen-reader evaluation. Use a coded prototype or working implementation where necessary, and identify what an earlier prototype can and cannot establish. HTML is an important foundation for web experiences, but content, dynamic behaviour, browser interaction and assistive technology also influence the result. Design intent must be verified in implementation.

## 21. Formal assurance and experienced accessibility

**Principle.** Evidence that a product satisfies specified requirements answers a bounded question. It does not, by itself, establish that people can use the product comfortably, confidently and effectively in their actual circumstances.

**Managerial application.** State what assurance covers, then investigate the intended experience. Evaluate meaningful tasks with relevant participants and examine completion, accuracy, independence, effort, recovery and satisfaction. Where a barrier remains outside an existing checklist, give it a named owner and assess its practical consequence rather than dismissing it as outside scope.

**Evidence and limits.** Avoid labelling an entire product accessible solely because one assessment passed. Equally, do not dismiss technical evaluation because lived-experience research is important. Different methods answer different questions. Describe the context, coverage and uncertainty of each conclusion, and investigate patterns hidden by aggregate results.

## 22. A foundation for continuing ambition

**Principle.** A documented baseline gives teams a shared starting point. An inclusive organisation continues learning from people and improving beyond what its existing requirements happen to describe.

**Managerial application.** Maintain internal guidance that explains intended outcomes, known barriers, useful patterns and evaluation approaches. Update it when research, incidents, changes in technology or repeated delivery problems reveal a gap. Connect each change to a reason so teams understand the judgement behind it. Give an owner responsibility for review and communicate changes to affected teams.

**Evidence and limits.** A baseline should remain useful and trustworthy; expanding it indefinitely can create contradictory or unmanageable instructions. Distinguish mandatory organisational requirements from recommendations and experiments. Preserve evidence and review dates, and explain exceptions. Maturity is demonstrated by better decisions and outcomes, not simply by a longer guidance document or a greater number of checks.

## 23. Auditing: purpose, timing and limits

**Principle.** Audits provide bounded assessments of condition, progress or consistency. They cannot carry the whole responsibility for accessibility throughout delivery.

**Managerial application.** Choose assessment scope and timing according to what needs to be learned: establishing a baseline, examining a supplier, reviewing a major change or investigating recurring barriers. Combine these assessments with appropriate checks during design, development and operation. Include changed content, integrated journeys and regressions, not just new components.

**Evidence and limits.** A periodic audit can support iterative delivery when its findings inform ongoing work. The problem is reliance on delayed discovery and repair. Automated checks, manual evaluation and user research provide different evidence; none replaces the others wholesale. Do not prescribe a universal automation percentage or reserve full assessments for an arbitrary calendar event. Explain coverage and avoid describing an assessment snapshot as a permanent guarantee.

## 24. Accessible components and design systems

**Principle.** Reusable components and patterns can preserve known solutions and reduce repeated work. Their value depends on implementation, documentation, adoption and use in context.

**Managerial application.** Document the problem a pattern addresses, the intended interaction, content responsibilities, relevant user needs, implementation guidance, reference behaviour and suitable tests. Explain permitted variations and combinations that need further evaluation. Give consuming teams a route to report problems and contribute research. Manage component changes and tell teams when an update affects their products.

**Evidence and limits.** A component working in isolation does not establish that a complete journey works. Composition, content, configuration and local modification can introduce new barriers. Measure adoption and recurring problems alongside user outcomes. Reuse solves common problems once where possible; it does not remove local responsibility. Establish improvement targets from observed conditions rather than promising a fixed percentage reduction in defects.

## 25. Structured content and machine interoperability

**Principle.** Clear structure, meaningful metadata and predictable platform behaviour can support both people and machine processing. These are related benefits of good implementation, not interchangeable objectives.

**Managerial application.** Preserve meaning in content and interaction rather than relying on visual presentation alone. Where relevant, examine how information is interpreted by browsers, assistive technology, search systems and other tools. Use resilient implementation approaches and consider the equipment and connectivity of the intended audience.

**Evidence and limits.** Do not promise improved search ranking, performance, internationalisation or AI training quality merely because an accessibility improvement was made. These outcomes have their own causes and require their own evaluation. More machine-readable information is not automatically available for reuse or training; permissions and privacy remain separate questions. Machine benefits can support an investment case while human participation remains the purpose of accessibility work.

## 26. Equivalence of experience in employment

**Principle.** Workplace inclusion depends on whether employees can perform the work for which they have the skills and aptitude. A system can obstruct that work through excessive effort, dependence, delay or error even when it has passed a formal assessment.

**Managerial application.** Evaluate representative work tasks using realistic configurations, integrations, access technologies, training and support. Examine independence, accuracy, fatigue, assistance, recovery and the flexibility to work effectively. Compare alternative systems and repeated tasks. Consider cumulative demands across the working day: small barriers in several systems may combine to undermine a role.

**Evidence and limits.** Equivalence means a fair and practicable opportunity to achieve the work outcome; it does not require identical techniques or identical completion times. Group averages can conceal serious individual barriers. Investigate meaningful differences and their causes before making procurement decisions. Require suppliers to provide relevant evidence, address barriers and support effective use; both supplier and employer retain responsibilities within their control. Use findings to improve systems and working arrangements, not to infer an employee's competence from an inaccessible task.

## 27. Accessibility policies, statements and delivery evidence

**Principle.** Distinguish what an organisation requires, what it currently delivers and what it plans to improve. Mixing these categories without explanation makes commitments difficult to trust.

**Managerial application.** Use a policy to establish commitments, responsibilities, decision rules and expectations. Use a statement to communicate the current position, known limitations and improvement plans. Maintain operational records of actions, ownership and evidence. These functions may sit in separate documents or clearly separated sections, provided readers can understand their status.

**Evidence and limits.** A policy may require a practice that is not yet consistently delivered; it should not claim the practice already happens everywhere. A statement should do more than express aspiration. Set review points appropriate to change and risk rather than assuming an annual review is always sufficient. Assign owners to commitments, be candid about gaps and distinguish a funded plan from an intention without resources.

## 28. Sustaining inclusion when priorities compete

**Principle.** Inclusion is most vulnerable when ownership, time and incentives are unclear. A stated priority becomes credible through what happens when deadlines, cost and user needs compete.

**Managerial application.** Give teams a way to raise a barrier, understand its consequences and reach someone with authority to act. Record decisions about unresolved barriers, including affected activities, available alternatives, action owner and review point. Involve relevant disabled people where their experience is needed to understand the consequence or evaluate a response. Recognise work on prevention and maintenance as well as visible launches.

**Evidence and limits.** Recording a decision does not resolve the barrier or make exclusion acceptable. Check whether promised actions occur and whether recurring exceptions reveal an underlying resourcing or design problem. Financial and efficiency benefits strengthen a business case, but support for inclusion should remain credible where a benefit cannot be reduced to a short-term commercial return.

# Putting the lessons into a delivery programme

## Early and continuous inclusion across the lifecycle

A programme needs connected decisions, not a separate accessibility activity at every stage. The following approach brings the lessons together. Adapt it to the delivery model, size of team and consequence of the work; it does not prescribe a particular sprint structure or organisational chart.

| Stage | Management focus | Practical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Discovery and framing | Establish intended participation, relevant contexts and who is missing from existing insight. | Audience and task definition; accessible research approach; known barriers and open questions. |
| Requirements and planning | Express what people need to accomplish and how success will be evaluated. | Outcome-based requirements; applicable acceptance criteria; ownership; suitable evaluation methods. |
| Design and architecture | Prevent avoidable barriers in journeys, structure, patterns and system dependencies. | Design rationale; evaluated options; research findings; documented interaction and content intent. |
| Implementation | Translate intent into working behaviour and preserve it through integration. | Suitable component use; semantic structure; interaction checks; relevant automated and human evaluation. |
| Pre-release evaluation | Assess the complete activity, address known barriers and understand remaining uncertainty. | Task and regression findings; relevant participant feedback; unresolved barrier decisions and action owners. |
| Operation and improvement | Learn from use, support, changes and incidents; prevent recurrence. | Experience measures; feedback routes; reviewed actions; updated patterns and guidance. |

## Frame requirements around participation

Start with the activity and the context, then identify the evidence needed. For example:

> An employee using their usual keyboard and screen-reader setup can locate a payslip, distinguish the relevant month and obtain the document without assistance or loss of information.

This describes an intended outcome. Implementation checks can examine structure, names and interaction, while evaluation of the complete task examines whether these choices work together. Emotional questions might examine confidence that the correct document was obtained and whether the employee had to disclose private information to seek help.

Each requirement should identify an accountable owner and an evaluation approach proportionate to its consequence. Automate checks where they reliably evaluate the relevant property. Use manual or assisted evaluation where judgement or interaction is involved, and participant research where lived experience or real use is needed. Some requirements will have no meaningful automated test. Do not invent one merely to populate a template.

## Use design systems to preserve decisions

A design system should explain why a pattern exists and what its users need, as well as how to implement it. A useful pattern record includes:

- The problem, intended activity and contexts of use.
- Relevant needs and the evidence that informed the approach.
- Information structure, content intent and interaction behaviour.
- Implementation guidance and a working reference where useful.
- Suitable evaluation scenarios, including known limitations.
- Permitted adaptations, ownership and change information.

Include brand choices, iconography, language, imagery and text alternatives where they affect the experience. Design systems support consistent judgement; they should not encourage teams to assume that combining approved parts automatically creates an inclusive journey.

## Use personas as prompts for investigation

Personas or functional-need scenarios can help teams consider needs that would otherwise be missed. They should represent meaningful activities, strategies and contexts rather than diagnoses attached to generic characters.

Choose coverage according to the experience being designed. There is no minimum persona count that establishes inclusion. A persona may prompt the question “Can someone complete this activity without relying on short-term memory?”, but only appropriate evaluation can show whether the proposed response works. Avoid treating personas as substitutes for disabled people or as permanent summaries of how a whole group behaves.

## Bring the right evidence into the right decision

Discovery research with disabled people can inform the problem and intended approach before a product exists. Evaluation of prototypes can examine emerging interaction choices. Evaluation of an integrated experience can investigate whether those decisions come together effectively.

Before asking participants to evaluate a developed experience, address known, avoidable barriers through appropriate design and technical checks. This makes better use of participants' time and allows research to explore unresolved experience questions. It does not require waiting for a flawless product before involving disabled people: early research, co-design and investigation of uncertain options have different purposes.

Define the research question, explain limitations and make the participation process accessible. Where a known barrier must be investigated directly, plan support and alternatives and be clear with participants about what they may encounter.

## Share responsibility without making it vague

Leadership should establish priorities and authority. Product and service owners should account for participation outcomes and unresolved risks. Design and content teams should communicate intended meaning and interaction. Engineering teams should implement and preserve behaviour. Quality teams should evaluate relevant properties and complete journeys. Researchers should investigate experience and communicate the limits of findings. Procurement and operational teams should examine supplier evidence and actual use.

Adapt these responsibilities to local roles, but identify a named accountable owner for each decision. Champions connect and support this work; specialists provide depth where needed. Neither arrangement removes the responsibility of the teams making and maintaining the experience.

## Choose measures that help managers act

A useful measure answers a decision question. “Can staff complete this task independently?” may require task observation. “Are our patterns preventing repeated implementation problems?” may require analysis of recurrence and adoption. “Does the experience preserve confidence?” requires asking people and understanding their accounts.

Use a combination of:

- **Outcome measures:** completion, accuracy, independence, sustainable effort, confidence and satisfaction in a defined context.
- **Process measures:** whether relevant responsibilities, checks and follow-up actions occur.
- **Learning measures:** unresolved questions, recurring barriers and whether findings change decisions.

Establish a baseline, explain the sample and identify the limitations. Compare like conditions where possible and examine differences within groups as well as between them. Do not interpret lower defect counts as improvement without considering detection coverage. Do not translate process completion into claims of equivalent experience.

## Protect people when collecting experience data

Collect disability, access-needs or identity information only for an explained purpose. Make disclosure voluntary and clarify how information will be used, who can access it and how long it will be retained. Provide a way to participate where possible without disclosing information unnecessary to the activity.

Segmentation should answer a relevant question. Broad social or disability groupings may help identify unequal outcomes; functional information may help explain an interaction barrier. Neither is a complete account of a person. Small groups, rare combinations and workplace roles can make people identifiable even when names are removed. Choose reporting detail accordingly and explain when limited coverage prevents a reliable conclusion. These are programme-level principles, not a complete data-protection procedure.

## Keep improvement work visible

For an unresolved barrier, record the affected activity, known consequence, available evidence, proposed response, accountable owner and review point. Explain any interim route and whether it creates additional effort, dependence or loss of privacy.

Prioritise according to consequences, including exclusion from essential work, repeated effort and cumulative burden. Revisit decisions when new evidence, usage or changes alter their significance. A delivery deadline does not make a barrier disappear, and a recorded exception should never be treated as a permanent exemption from improvement.

# Instruction / input / output examples

These examples illustrate application of the lessons. They do not add requirements beyond the principles and their stated scope. They may be used as model-behaviour examples alongside the explanatory sections. They are not assertions that a model has been trained or that a short example can replace a complete programme.

## Example 1. Emotional and functional experience

### Instruction

Explain why successful completion alone does not establish an inclusive experience.

### Input

A service team says its alternative telephone route is sufficient because every customer can obtain the same information.

### Output

Examine what using that route requires. A customer may obtain the information while losing privacy, independence, convenience or the ability to act at a preferred time. Ask affected people about the experience and compare the complete activity, including waiting and assistance. Investigate an independent route where possible and improve the interim service. Do not assume that the same final information creates equivalent participation, or claim a particular response is adequate without evidence.

## Example 2. Strategy and ownership

### Instruction

Translate an organisational inclusion commitment into managerial action.

### Input

The organisation promises that its products “work for everyone”, but inclusion is assigned only to an accessibility adviser.

### Output

Treat the commitment as a responsibility shared through delivery. Identify who makes decisions about audience, research, procurement, design, implementation and release, then give those owners access to relevant expertise and evidence. The adviser can support and challenge decisions, but cannot own every product outcome. Establish measures that reflect the intended activities and known barriers. Report gaps between the promise and current delivery without treating the promise itself as evidence of success.

## Example 3. Research access and sampling

### Instruction

Help a research manager respond to an inaccessible recruitment method.

### Input

The only route into a study is a survey that some screen-reader users cannot complete. The manager proposes making 20% of places available to disabled people.

### Output

First make recruitment and participation possible through accessible methods. A quota cannot correct a route that prevents people joining. Define the experiences and contexts needed to answer the research question, then select an appropriate sampling approach. Record which people were reached and which remain absent. Treat any local numerical target as a justified planning choice, not a universal rule or proof that the findings represent the entire audience.

## Example 4. Evidence from one participant

### Instruction

Distinguish evidence of a barrier from evidence of its prevalence.

### Input

One participant cannot complete a critical task. A stakeholder says no action is justified until ten people report the same problem.

### Output

The observation is evidence of what happened in that context. Investigate the interaction and determine whether the barrier can be reproduced or otherwise explained. A clear failure may justify correction immediately. Further research may be useful to understand scope, causes or alternative solutions, but there is no fixed confirmation count required before evidence matters. Report separately what is known about the barrier and what remains unknown about its prevalence.

## Example 5. Audience scope

### Instruction

Challenge an exclusion decision without assuming every scope choice is illegitimate.

### Input

A team lists screen-reader users as outside its audience because its chosen interface library does not support them.

### Output

That is an implementation constraint being presented as an audience decision. Revisit the intended purpose and who should be able to participate. Examine alternative patterns, libraries or implementation approaches, and identify the consequences of continuing with the restriction. Give the decision an accountable owner and review route. Recording the group on an exclusion list does not justify the barrier or make the product inclusive.

## Example 6. Evaluation during iterative delivery

### Instruction

Recommend an evaluation approach that supports continuous delivery.

### Input

A team wants to replace all ongoing evaluation with an annual full audit.

### Output

An annual assessment can provide useful assurance, but it leaves a long gap between decisions and feedback. Build suitable automated and human checks into changes, include complete journeys and relevant regressions, and use research to investigate experience. Choose additional audits according to risk, change and the questions that need answering. State what each method covers and what it cannot establish; do not promise a universal proportion of automated coverage.

## Example 7. Design-system reuse

### Instruction

Explain the limits of an approved component.

### Input

An engineer says no further evaluation is needed because every component in the journey comes from the design system.

### Output

The components provide a useful foundation, but their combination, content, configuration and integration determine the journey. Check the working activity and any local changes against the intended behaviour. Use component evidence where it remains applicable and focus additional evaluation on the complete experience and the risks introduced by context. Feed recurring problems back to the design-system owner so other teams benefit from the learning.

## Example 8. Workplace equivalence

### Instruction

Interpret a difference in workplace task performance fairly.

### Input

Disabled staff take longer on a recurring administrative task, although the supplier has provided formal assurance.

### Output

Investigate the actual work: setup, navigation, errors, assistance, fatigue and repetition across the day. Examine whether the difference arises from avoidable design or integration barriers, while accounting for relevant differences in context and familiarity. Do not infer employee capability from the task or require identical completion times. Assess practical effects on independence and sustainable work, compare alternatives and agree improvements with the supplier and employer. Formal assurance is useful evidence, but it does not resolve the reported experience.

## Example 9. Choosing a typeface

### Instruction

Guide a design manager evaluating competing typography choices.

### Input

One candidate font gives the best average reading speed, but some participants find the text tiring and struggle to distinguish characters.

### Output

Do not use the average alone to choose. Examine the affected tasks, participant experiences and relevant letterforms, together with size, spacing, rendering and content. Consider adjustment options or a different typographic system. Explain trade-offs and investigate serious difficulties before selecting a default. The objective is usable reading in context, not declaring one font universally accessible.

## Example 10. Current position and future commitment

### Instruction

Separate policy commitments from claims about delivery.

### Input

An organisation's policy requires inclusive research, but several teams have not yet established accessible recruitment.

### Output

Retain a clear policy requirement and describe the delivery gap honestly. Identify the teams and participation routes affected, assign responsibility and resource an improvement plan. Public or internal statements about the current position should distinguish functioning practice from planned change. Monitor whether participation becomes possible; publishing the policy or completing training does not demonstrate that the gap has closed.

# Knowledge-base maintenance

## Preserve meaning through retrieval and reuse

Keep each lesson's principle, application and evidence limits together where possible. If content must be divided into smaller chunks, retain the topic title, scope and limitations or a dependable link to that context. Keep examples associated with the principles they illustrate.

Do not strengthen “may” into “must”, omit qualifications when quoting a lesson, or generate numerical thresholds absent from the source. When applying a lesson, explain the relevant context and any assumptions. Where the available material cannot support a conclusion, state the uncertainty and identify the evidence needed.

## Review ownership and change

Assign an owner to maintain the knowledge base and a review point suited to its use. Review sooner when practice reveals an ambiguity or contradiction. Check principles, examples and operational guidance together so that an edit in one layer does not leave another giving different advice.

Keep provenance distinct from guidance. Attribution identifies the source of the thinking; it does not establish that every suggested implementation has been researched or proven. Communicate material changes to people or systems relying on the knowledge base.
